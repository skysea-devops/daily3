When we think of Halloween today, we tend to picture round, grinning orange pumpkins. The Ghost Turnip at the National Museum of Ireland offers a very different face. Its hollow eyes, twisted mouth and uneven teeth are more unsettling than cheerful. The object on display is a painted plaster model, made by museum artist Eileen Barnes to preserve the appearance of a lantern once carved from a turnip. In 1943, a schoolteacher from County Donegal, Rois Ní Braonáin, brought the tradition to the museum's attention, explaining that such lanterns had been familiar in her area around 1900.

The contrast with the bright pumpkins outside American homes is striking. How did an old Irish custom become one of the most recognisable symbols of an American holiday? And how much of Halloween's earlier life survives in the celebration we know today?

## Before Halloween Had a Name

Long before Halloween acquired its modern name, the end of October marked a change in the year. In Ireland and other parts of the Celtic world, Samhain was associated with the end of summer and the beginning of winter. The harvest was coming to a close. The shorter days brought the colder, darker months, when food, shelter and the next season's prospects mattered greatly.

Samhain also came to be associated with the supernatural. In Irish folklore, this was a time when the usual boundaries between the living and the dead were thought to be less certain. Spirits might return, and people took precautions against unwelcome visitors. Yet the surviving evidence does not give us a complete picture of what people did in the distant pre-Christian past. It would be misleading to imagine that every familiar Halloween custom, including the carved turnip, already existed in ancient Celtic times.

Christian observances added another layer. November 1 became All Saints' Day, and November 2 was associated with prayers for the dead on All Souls' Day. The evening before All Saints' Day was known as All Hallows' Eve, from which the word Halloween developed. Older seasonal customs and Christian practices existed alongside one another, but their relationship was neither simple nor uniform. There is no need to make Samhain and modern Halloween into a single, unbroken tradition to recognise the connections between them.

By the eighteenth century, we can see Halloween customs much more clearly. In his 1785 poem Halloween, the Scottish poet Robert Burns describes country people gathering for games and attempts to predict the future. Young men and women pull plants, burn nuts and look for clues about whom they might marry. Burns writes of neighbours coming together:

> Some merry, friendly, countra-folks / Together did convene
>
> — Robert Burns, Halloween (1785), stanza 2

There are fairies and supernatural beliefs in the poem, but there is also laughter, flirtation and curiosity about the future. Halloween was not simply a night to fear the dead. It was an occasion for people to meet, play games and share old beliefs.

Some of these customs would cross the Atlantic with the people who practised them.

## Crossing the Atlantic

Halloween did not arrive in North America all at once. Some English settlers had already brought seasonal games, stories and customs to the colonies, although Halloween itself was not widely celebrated there. In her 1919 book The Book of Hallowe'en, Ruth Edna Kelley noted that certain older activities, such as games with apples and attempts to discover a future partner, had persisted in colonial America.

The larger change came through immigration, especially during the nineteenth century. Irish and Scottish newcomers carried their own customs into towns and cities across North America. They brought familiar ways of marking the end of October: visiting neighbours, telling ghost stories, playing fortune-telling games and gathering with friends.

For many Irish families, the journey was not simply a search for new opportunities. The Great Famine, beginning in 1845, brought widespread hunger, death and displacement. Large numbers left Ireland in the following years, many travelling to the United States and Canada. What they carried was not limited to the possessions they could pack. Festivals, songs and familiar household customs remained part of life even after they had left home.

These practices did not remain confined to immigrant families. Neighbours encountered them, communities adopted them, and different customs began to mix. Halloween was becoming familiar in places where it had once been a relatively minor occasion.

Kelley's book captures that changing world. Published in 1919, it devoted separate chapters to Irish and Scottish Halloween customs before turning to their American counterparts. It describes a celebration that already included parties, games, ghost stories and carved pumpkins. By then, Halloween was no longer simply a collection of traditions remembered by immigrant communities. It had become part of a wider American social calendar.

As the celebration spread, its customs began to take on new forms. The clearest example was the lantern.

## From Turnips to Pumpkins

In parts of Ireland, people carved frightening faces into turnips, hollowed them out and placed candles inside. The lanterns could be set out on Halloween night to startle neighbours. The National Museum of Ireland's Ghost Turnip preserves the appearance of one such design: a narrow, grimacing face that looks very different from the pumpkins we see today.

The name jack-o'-lantern became associated with these lights and with stories about a wandering figure called Jack. In one well-known Irish tale, Jack is refused entry to both heaven and hell and must wander with a light of his own. The folklore around the name developed over time; it should not be treated as a single explanation for every lantern-making custom.

Carving a turnip takes effort. Its flesh is hard, and there is little room inside. Pumpkins, native to the Americas, offered a different material. They were larger, easier to hollow out and well suited to making a lantern. As Halloween customs spread in North America, pumpkins became a familiar choice.

This was a small practical change with a lasting visual result. A lantern that had been made from a pale, rough root vegetable could now be made from a large orange pumpkin. The shape was easier to recognise from a distance, and the face could be cut in broad, simple lines. A custom associated with Ireland gradually acquired the appearance by which Halloween is now known around the world.

![A white house at dusk with its porch and front garden lined with glowing jack-o'-lanterns.](/essays/how-halloween-crossed-the-atlantic/halloween-house-pumpkins.jpg "Halloween house in Romeo, Michigan, photograph by Bruno Guerrero, 2025 — Unsplash.")

Kelley was already describing candlelit pumpkins as a feature of American Halloween parties in 1919. By that point, the pumpkin had become far more than an alternative to the turnip. It was becoming a symbol of the holiday itself.

But the lantern was only one part of a much larger transformation. Halloween's games, visitors and occasional mischief were changing too.

## The Making of an American Holiday

In 1894, a group of roughly two hundred boys in Washington, D.C., reportedly threw bags of flour at well-dressed passengers on streetcars. It was one of many Halloween incidents recorded in the American press over the years. Other pranks included rattling windows, moving gates and damaging property. What could be playful to those taking part was not always amusing to the people on the receiving end.

This was another side of American Halloween. Alongside indoor parties and fortune-telling games, the night offered young people a chance to behave in ways that would have been discouraged on an ordinary evening. As towns and cities grew, some of these customs became harder for neighbours to ignore. A prank that once seemed harmless could become expensive or dangerous.

Families, schools and local organisations increasingly tried to make Halloween a more organised occasion. Parties, community events and activities for children offered alternatives to destructive mischief. Newspapers and civic groups also encouraged celebrations that brought neighbours together rather than setting them against one another. The change did not happen everywhere at the same time, and older habits did not disappear overnight.

Meanwhile, door-to-door visiting already had a history of its own. In Scotland and Ireland, guising involved people dressing up and sometimes performing a song, joke or verse in exchange for food or small gifts. The Christian custom of souling, in which visitors received food in return for prayers for the dead, offered another point of comparison. These practices did not turn directly into modern trick-or-treating in a single step, but they help explain why costumes, visits and treats came to belong together.

During the first half of the twentieth century, American children increasingly began going from door to door in costume. Trick-or-treating became especially widespread after the Second World War. Wartime sugar restrictions had ended, growing suburbs provided neighbourhood routes, and families with young children took part in organised local celebrations. Individually wrapped sweets made it simple to offer something to visitors without preparing a special game or performance.

![A child in a cardboard box costume carrying a bag up a driveway past Halloween decorations.](/essays/how-halloween-crossed-the-atlantic/trick-or-treater.jpg "A trick-or-treater on Halloween — Wikimedia Commons.")

Businesses recognised the opportunity. Sweet manufacturers promoted products for Halloween, while costume makers supplied outfits that families could buy rather than make at home. Over time, commercial products became a familiar part of the celebration.

Yet companies did not create Halloween from nothing. They entered a holiday already shaped by immigrants, local customs, children, parents and neighbourhoods. The celebration that emerged combined older practices with newer habits. A night of fortune-telling, disguises, ghost stories and occasional trouble had become, for many American families, an evening of costumes, illuminated pumpkins and children asking for sweets.

## What Survives the Journey

The grimacing turnip remembered in County Donegal and the orange pumpkin on an American doorstep belong to the same long story. One was hard to carve, the other easier to hollow out. One was familiar in parts of Ireland, while the other became a widely recognised symbol in North America. Their faces look different, but both are reminders of the people who carried a custom with them and adapted it to a new place.

A tradition does not always survive by remaining unchanged. Some of Halloween's old games and beliefs have faded, while others have found a place in new forms of celebration. What began as a set of local seasonal customs became part of the lives of people far from their original homes. The two lanterns, made from different vegetables on opposite sides of the Atlantic, still hold a light inside.
