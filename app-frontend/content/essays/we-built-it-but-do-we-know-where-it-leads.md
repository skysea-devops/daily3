In Chapter IV of H. G. Wells's 1904 novel The Food of the Gods and How It Came to Earth, the chemist Mr Bensington hesitates over the unpredictable future effects of a discovery he has helped bring about. Turning to his colleague Cossar, he asks:

> "After all, ought we to go on with it?"
>
> — H. G. Wells, The Food of the Gods, Book I, Chapter IV

What makes this question striking is the moment in which it is asked. The discovery has already been made. The substance works. The question is no longer what science can achieve, but whether they should continue.

When I read that line, artificial intelligence came to mind. It seems we are living through something like Bensington's moment of hesitation. For years, AI was a question about the limits of engineering and imagination. Could a machine recognise complex patterns, write coherent texts, or perform tasks we considered uniquely human? Today, we can sit in front of a screen and watch a model write code, bring together research from many papers, or respond to subtle differences in language. Much of this has become ordinary at a surprising speed.

We have built something extraordinary, and its development is moving fast. Yet Bensington's question stays with me. Being able to make something does not mean we understand where it will lead.

## The Promise of a Discovery

The two scientists in Wells's story develop a substance called Herakleophorbia IV, which allows living things to grow far beyond their normal size. Bensington calls it the Food of the Gods. The discovery brings genuine excitement. What had seemed to be a biological limit has been overcome.

![Students working in a bacteriology laboratory at Howard University around 1900.](/essays/we-built-it-but-do-we-know-where-it-leads/howard-bacteriology-1900.jpg "A bacteriology class at Howard University, Washington, D.C., around 1900 — Library of Congress. Public domain.")

Even imagining the possible benefits of this Food is extraordinary. Chickens and crops could grow to enormous sizes, and farms could produce much more food. Far stronger human beings might reshape the world.

Yet the two scientists remain focused on the experiment itself: the growth curves, the substance they have developed, and the evidence that it works.

What Bensington and Redwood cannot know is what will happen when their discovery leaves the conditions in which it was tested and enters the ordinary world, where habits, mistakes, ambitions, and fears shape human behaviour. Their success in the laboratory tells them far less about that world than they had hoped.

## Is Uncertainty a Reason to Stop?

The Food is taken from the laboratory to an experimental farm, where it is fed to chickens. The people caring for the farm handle the substance carelessly. Soon its effects spread beyond the intended subjects. Giant rats, wasps, and even giant humans appear. The new discovery is becoming dangerous to ordinary people.

AI is crossing a similar threshold. When a powerful capability leaves a research setting and enters everyday life, it passes into the hands of people and institutions with different priorities and different levels of understanding. Users may push it in directions its developers never imagined; institutions may reshape their routines around it; both its benefits and its errors may spread far beyond the original setting.

At this point, we should ask:

> Is uncertainty itself a sufficient reason to stop progress?

Does the possibility of losing control over how an innovation or new technology is used, or being unable to foresee its future, make all its other potential uses worthless?

In the novel, Bensington has a valid reason to hesitate. The scientists do not know all the effects of the Food. If they continue, people who have had no say in the decision could be harmed.

AI raises a similar problem. We are already using it at work, in education, and in healthcare, even as we are still learning where it makes mistakes and how it affects our decisions. Those mistakes could affect someone's income or safety. There is also the risk that we begin to rely on these systems without questioning them.

And there is another issue: even when a technology brings benefits, they may not be shared equally.

## A Better Future, But for Whom?

The Food in Wells's novel opens the door to a different kind of human life. The children who consume it grow into giants, with bodies and ambitions that no longer fit the world around them. Those who remain small must live with a transformation they did not choose. For the giants, the old world feels like a constraint, while for others, the new world is threatening.

I see a similar tension in the promises made about AI. It could improve research, education, healthcare, and the way we work. But the resources needed to develop the most powerful systems are not equally accessible to everyone.

Structural risks remain in areas such as computing infrastructure, hardware, and data. These advantages can be difficult for new players to replicate and may further strengthen companies and countries that are already in powerful positions.

![The Summit supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in 2018.](/essays/we-built-it-but-do-we-know-where-it-leads/summit-supercomputer-2018.jpg "Summit supercomputer, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, 2018 — Carlos Jones / ORNL, CC BY 2.0.")

New tools can widen access to knowledge and productive work, while the resources behind those tools remain concentrated in a few hands. The same progress that gives one person more independence may make another more dependent on systems they do not own or control.

What troubles me is the possibility that the people who benefit most from a transformation may not always be the ones who bear its greatest risks. If a technology changes how we work, learn, and make decisions, we cannot judge it only by what it can do. We must also question on whose behalf it makes decisions, according to whose values, and under whose control.

## Living With the Consequences

Towards the end of The Food of the Gods, Professor Redwood visits the place where the giants have gathered. He sees the scale of what his generation has built and realises that the world he helped bring into being has grown beyond him. Turning to Cossar, he says:

> "We have made a new world, and it isn't ours."
>
> — H. G. Wells, The Food of the Gods, Book III, Chapter V

Redwood realises that the world they created is no longer taking shape according to their expectations. The change they once set in motion is now moving towards a future independent of them. In much the same way, artificial intelligence is now shaping a future we cannot fully predict.

Artificial intelligence is already changing how we work, learn, and make decisions. Its possibilities are remarkable, but we still know very little about how these changes will shape our lives in the long run. Some people may gain opportunities they never had before, while others may find themselves at a disadvantage. And those who develop and control the technology may not be the ones who experience its greatest consequences.

The decisions being made today will affect people who have little say in making them.

If progress promises a better future, who gets to decide which risks are worth taking—and who must bear the consequences?
