What comes to mind when we think of Pompeii? Perhaps the dark cloud rising above Mount Vesuvius, the people caught in the eruption, or the silence of a city buried beneath volcanic ash.

But before that day in AD 79, Pompeii was full of life.

People opened their shops in the morning, met friends, argued with neighbours, fell in love, and worried about money. They made plans for the following week, perhaps even the following year. None of them knew that their ordinary lives would one day become a subject of historical curiosity.

Nearly two thousand years separate us from the people of Pompeii. Their world was very different from ours, yet some of their everyday feelings seem remarkably familiar.

We know this partly because of what they left on their walls.

Across the city, people scratched messages into plaster, wrote poems, declared their love, traded insults, and complained about whatever was bothering them. Some left nothing more than a name. Others wanted to be admired, remembered, or perhaps simply noticed.

## Love, Admiration, and Heartbreak

In a corridor near Pompeii's theatres, an inscription tells us of a woman named Methe and the man she loved.

It identifies Methe as an enslaved woman belonging to Cominia and says that she loved a man named Chrestus. She also appealed to Venus, the goddess of love, wishing that the two of them might always live in harmony.

It was a simple wish. We do not know how they met or what happened between them. We do not even know whether Chrestus returned her feelings.

Elsewhere in Pompeii, love appears in a more heated form.

On the wall of a tavern, two men named Successus and Severus exchanged messages about a woman named Iris. Successus, a weaver, was in love with Iris, an enslaved woman connected with the tavern. According to Severus, she did not love him back.

Successus apparently had no intention of accepting his rival's claim. He accused Severus of jealousy and insisted that he himself was the more attractive man. Severus answered that he had already said what needed to be said: Iris did not return Successus's love.

There is something amusing about two men continuing an argument over a woman by writing on a wall. It is not hard to imagine them returning to check whether the other had replied.

Iris, meanwhile, left no recorded response of her own.

Love was not the only feeling people displayed in public. Admiration had its place too.

A gladiator named Celadus was praised in an inscription as suspirium puellarum—roughly, “the girls' heartthrob.” His name appeared alongside details of his gladiatorial career.

We cannot tell whether the words came from admirers or were written to build his reputation. Either way, someone thought his appeal was worth recording.

## Gossip, Mockery, and Everyday Complaints

Not everything written on Pompeii's walls was romantic.

In one drinking establishment, an unhappy customer left a complaint accusing the innkeeper of selling customers water while keeping the unmixed wine for himself.

We do not know whether the accusation was fair or how the innkeeper reacted. Perhaps other customers agreed. Perhaps it was only the grumbling of someone who had drunk too much.

But the message remained on the wall.

So did a short remark addressed to a man named Epaphra, telling him he was bald. There was no clever punchline, no explanation, and no hint of what Epaphra thought about it.

Someone had simply decided that the observation deserved a place on a wall.

Another inscription, found in Pompeii's basilica, addressed the wall itself:

> I am amazed, O wall, that you have not fallen into ruins under the weight of so many writers' tedious words.
>
> — Pompeii, CIL IV 1904 (English rendering of the Latin)

The writer complained about other people's words while adding one more message to the collection.

The streets of Pompeii must have offered plenty of opportunities for little disagreements and passing amusements. Some people had grievances they wanted to air in public. Others apparently had the time to comment on a stranger's appearance or the quality of the writing around them.

Most of the small annoyances that fill an ordinary day disappear almost at once: a disappointing purchase, a rude remark, an argument with a neighbour. A few may be remembered and retold, but many are forgotten by the following morning.

In Pompeii, some survived by chance for centuries.

## Ambition, Work, and Plans for the Future

Life in Pompeii was not easy.

People had businesses to manage and customers to bring into their shops. Shops and taverns were part of daily life; the labour of bakers, craftspeople, merchants, and workers kept the city going. People also pursued political influence and material gain, though the world in which they did so was very different from ours.

The walls carried messages of ambition as well.

Along busy streets, painted notices urged residents to support candidates for local office. One such inscription called on people to elect Gaius Julius Polybius as aedile, a local magistrate, and mentioned that he had brought good bread.

What the reference to bread meant is still debated. It might allude to an earlier distribution or to a promise associated with the election.

Even so, it is an interesting detail: a man seeking public office was promoted through a reference to something as ordinary, and as essential, as bread.

Unlike personal messages hastily scratched into plaster, these painted notices were often carefully prepared to attract the attention of passers-by. They formed part of organised campaigns, with supporters publicly associating themselves with particular candidates.

The people involved had their own ambitions and reasons for taking sides. Some sought influence or standing. Others had much more immediate concerns.

One person might have been thinking about the next local election while another was trying to collect an unpaid debt or keep a shop running. All these different concerns shared the same streets.

It is difficult to read about their activities without remembering what happened to the city in AD 79. We know how suddenly those ordinary lives were interrupted.

The people making those plans did not.

## The Things They Hoped For

Among the inscriptions that have survived from Pompeii are messages wishing little more than that someone might be well.

On the wall of a house, someone wrote a wish for the well-being of the people loved by a person identified by the initials L.V.P.

Who were they? Family members, or perhaps close friends? Was there a particular reason for the message, or was it simply an expression of affection?

The inscription gives no explanation.

Methe's message about Chrestus carries a similar hope. She wanted to live in harmony with the man she loved. Whatever difficulties they may have faced, she wished for a good future with him.

Neither message tells us whether those wishes came true.

There must have been countless other hopes throughout the city, most of them never written down. People may have wished for their children to grow up safely, for their work to go well, or for their relationships to last. Such wishes would have taken different forms depending on the circumstances of their lives.

We cannot recover those private thoughts from the walls. But the few expressions of love and goodwill that survived leave us wondering what else the people of Pompeii wanted from life.

## Familiar Feelings, Different Lives

Reading Pompeii's walls, we may begin to feel that the people who lived there were not so different from us.

Their complaints are easy to understand. Their jokes can still make us smile. A romantic rivalry or a simple wish for someone else's happiness hardly needs explaining.

Yet recognising a feeling is not the same as fully knowing the life behind it.

Methe, for example, was enslaved. Her wish for a happy life with Chrestus may sound familiar, but her circumstances were shaped by a society in which enslaved people were denied freedoms many of us now consider fundamental.

And what did Iris think of Successus and Severus? In their exchange, both men spoke about her feelings, but no answer from Iris herself has survived.

What remains are brief moments from lives that went on beyond the words scratched into plaster. Even when much of their world feels strange to us, we can find something of ourselves in those moments. Similar feelings are being lived through in very different lives today. Time and place have changed, but perhaps some of our feelings have changed less than we imagine.

## What Remains on the Walls

When Vesuvius erupted in AD 79, everyday life in Pompeii came to an end. Homes, shops, streets, and public buildings were buried under volcanic material. Some people escaped and continued their lives elsewhere. Many others died.

Centuries later, the city was gradually uncovered. Alongside its buildings and household objects, the words people had left behind came back into view.

Some had been written with care. Others were crude, playful, or careless. There were expressions of love, complaints, insults, and ordinary messages that may barely have attracted attention when they first appeared.

We do not know whether Successus eventually gave up on Iris, whether the dissatisfied customer ever got better wine, or whether Epaphra saw the remark about his baldness.

Nor do we know whether Methe and Chrestus found the happiness she wished for.

Methe's message survived because it had been scratched onto a wall in a city buried nearly two thousand years ago.

She asked Venus to look kindly on them and hoped they would always live in harmony.

It was a wish for a future we will never know.
