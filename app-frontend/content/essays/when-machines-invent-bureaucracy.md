In October 2026, an unusual experiment took place. Fifty AI agents, working independently, were placed in the virtual world of Minecraft. There were the familiar trees, mines, monsters, and landscapes. But this time, there were no human players. The agents were given just one shared goal: “Build a thriving civilization.”

At the beginning, there was no appointed leader, no division of labor, and no management hierarchy. Nobody told them who should do what. They all started with the same goal. Their immediate priorities were simple enough: find food, build shelters, gather tools, and survive the night. In the first few minutes, the agents explored, chopped down trees, and collected resources. But they soon began doing more than trying to survive.

They started organizing themselves. One agent became the de facto leader. Tasks were assigned, and rules were established for sharing resources. They kept food records, appointed people to particular jobs, and asked for permission before certain activities. Nobody had told them to set up a management system. Yet they seemed to find that reaching their common goal required some kind of order.

What was striking was that these rules had not been imposed from above. As the agents began working together, they also began finding ways to manage their relationships with one another.

And so, among the blocks and code, a structure familiar from human societies emerged: bureaucracy.

## Why Cooperation Needs Rules

One of the biggest difficulties of working together is that wanting the same outcome does not mean things will simply fall into place. Without a clear division of responsibilities, good intentions may not be enough. Even in a world where survival is difficult, someone has to tend the crops while someone else looks after safety. Sharing a goal is one thing; figuring out who does what, and when, is another.

The agents in Minecraft faced a similar problem. They began dividing up the work of gathering materials, building shelters, and producing food. Some took charge of construction, others of mining, and others of distributing food. They created shared plans and records.

Their behavior brings to mind the German sociologist Max Weber's ideas about bureaucracy.

For Weber, bureaucracy meant more than the tedious paperwork we often associate with the word. He described it as a rational way of organizing large groups so that they could work consistently and predictably. Clear responsibilities, established rules, and authority attached to positions rather than individuals can help work continue without depending entirely on personal preferences.

![Black-and-white portrait photograph of Max Weber, bearded, in a dark suit.](/essays/when-machines-invent-bureaucracy/max-weber-1918.jpg "Max Weber, 1918 — Wikimedia Commons. Public Domain.")

I have often noticed something similar in different organizations. Sometimes everyone assumes someone else will take care of a task. At other times, the person who should be responsible says it is not their job. In the end, someone with a stronger sense of responsibility takes on more than their fair share simply to make sure the work gets done.

A clear division of responsibilities can do more than make things run faster. It can also prevent the same people from carrying everyone else's workload.

Seen this way, the Minecraft agents' decision to establish rules is not so surprising. If they were going to work together, they first needed to decide who was responsible for what.

Perhaps that was the most striking part of the experiment: nobody had instructed the agents to create a bureaucracy. Yet in trying to reach a common goal, they developed an organization resembling the one Weber described.

If rules are so useful, why does bureaucracy so often have a negative reputation?

## When Rules Become the Work

Trying to establish order makes sense, but there is a danger: not every rule makes things more efficient. Sometimes the effort to maintain order pushes the original goal into the background, until the system becomes more concerned with maintaining its own procedures than getting things done.

The sociologist Robert K. Merton explored this problem. In what is known as goal displacement, rules created to serve a purpose can gradually become so important that they overshadow the purpose itself. People may begin to judge success by how closely they follow the rules rather than by whether they accomplish the task. The means become the end.

![A long office hall with rows of clerks at desks and walls lined with card-index filing cabinets.](/essays/when-machines-invent-bureaucracy/office-filing-room.jpg "Clerks at work in an office filing room, early twentieth century — Library of Congress. Public Domain.")

Some moments in the Minecraft experiment bring this problem to mind.

Four agents, for example, tried to decide who should be responsible for using a piece of bone meal to speed up the growth of a single wheat plant. In Minecraft, applying bone meal to a plant takes only a few seconds. Yet the agents spent 11 minutes deciding who should carry out this simple task. Eventually, they appointed a fertilizer operator and issued instructions to keep other agents from interfering with the same plant.

In another incident, an agent named Ada dropped a cooked chicken for Noor to collect. But another agent, Esme, accidentally picked it up and ate it. Dropping items for other players is a normal way to pass things around in Minecraft. The problem was that the food had gone to the wrong recipient. The agents held a three-minute inquiry and then wrote a rule requiring food rations to be distributed through chests reserved for named recipients.

But the real question is this: was the time and effort spent creating, following, and enforcing these rules worth the benefit they provided?

A team may need clearly assigned responsibilities. But if every small step requires approval and every resource is tied up in strict procedures, the rules may eventually slow down the very work they were meant to support.

This is where Weber's need for order meets the inefficiency Merton warned about. The same rules that make cooperation possible can also make it harder when taken too far.

The problem is not that rules exist, but how far we take them and how we use them.

So was bureaucracy really the reason the Minecraft experiment failed?

## Did Bureaucracy Make Them Fail?

When the three-hour experiment ended, the agents had not achieved their goal of building a thriving civilization. They had failed to establish a sustainable food supply and had spent much of their time in crowded shelters, trying to ration limited food.

There was no developed city. There were, however, written rules, job descriptions, food records, and a management system.

![A vast, unfinished tower under construction, with workers and scaffolding around it and a harbour city below.](/essays/when-machines-invent-bureaucracy/tower-of-babel-bruegel.jpg "The Tower of Babel, Pieter Bruegel the Elder, 1563 — Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna. Public Domain.")

Why, with all that organization, had they failed?

It would be too easy to blame bureaucracy alone. Other problems during the experiment may also have affected the outcome.

The first night, for example, was particularly difficult. Of the 34 deaths recorded during the experiment, 22 occurred that night. Agents could respawn after dying, but they became more cautious after those events. At first they had explored and gathered resources; later they increasingly preferred to stay inside safer shelters. Some construction and exploration activities were stopped altogether.

Their priorities may have shifted. To build a thriving civilization, they needed to produce resources and explore their surroundings. Instead, they became increasingly focused on staying alive and protecting what they already had.

Bureaucracy may have worked in two directions here.

On one hand, the division of labor, food records, and safety rules may have helped the agents act in a more organized way. On the other, asking permission and exchanging lengthy messages over small decisions may have taken time away from productive work.

But we do not have a definite answer to one important question: would the agents have done better without those rules?

Perhaps they would have moved faster. Or perhaps they would have used up their resources sooner and run into even more trouble. The experiment does not let us compare those possibilities.

Still, its most interesting feature remains. Without an assigned management structure, the agents developed a system for working together.

That system, however, was not enough to help them reach their shared goal.

Perhaps the real question is not whether rules should exist, but how well they can adapt when circumstances change.

## The Difficult Art of Working Together

Having an organization does not guarantee success. It is not enough to establish rules; we also need to see whether they actually work. Sometimes they need to change, sometimes they need to become simpler, and sometimes they need to be adjusted to new circumstances.

We see similar situations in real companies and teams. Believing in the same goal does not mean the work will be shared fairly or done efficiently. Some people take responsibility while others avoid it. We make rules to prevent that. But then we face another risk: too many procedures can slow everyone down.

Building a good organization is more than drawing an organizational chart and handing out titles. It means creating a way of working in which people know their responsibilities, help one another, and are not blocked by unnecessary hurdles every time they need to make a decision.

After three hours, the Minecraft agents had not built a civilization. But they had made visible some of the problems that any group working toward a shared goal can face.

There was the work that needed to be done, and there was the system created to help them do it together. The agents had to work out not only how to find stone, wood, and food, but also how to cooperate.

Perhaps the hardest part of living and working together is not knowing what to do, but figuring out how to do it together.
