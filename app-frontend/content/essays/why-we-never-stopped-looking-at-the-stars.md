A spacecraft returns from orbit, passes through Earth's atmosphere, and approaches a launch tower in Texas. As it slows, two enormous mechanical arms close around it, catching it in midair. On November 14, 2026, SpaceX achieved this with the upper stage of its Starship rocket for the first time.

It is the kind of sight that makes us think about the limits of engineering, journeys to Mars, and how far space technology has come. We can send machines beyond our atmosphere and bring some of them back intact. But our interest in the sky did not begin with rockets. Thousands of years before modern instruments existed, people were already watching the stars. What they hoped to find there changed from one age to another.

What did people look for in the stars before they could even imagine reaching them?

## When the Stars Kept Time

For early settled communities, looking at the sky was more than an abstract curiosity. It was one way to keep track of time. Without printed calendars, people could watch the changing positions of stars and the return of familiar patterns in the sky to mark the passing seasons.

One of the clearest examples comes from ancient Mesopotamia. A collection of astronomical texts known as MUL.APIN, probably compiled around 1000 BCE from earlier observations, shows how carefully people recorded the sky. Its name comes from the opening reference to the Plough Star. The tablets list stars and constellations and note when certain stars first appeared on the eastern horizon before sunrise.

By observing these appearances, Mesopotamian skywatchers could follow the turning of the year. Their records also helped them relate the lunar calendar to the solar year. For societies whose lives depended on seasonal work, this knowledge mattered. Knowing when a season was approaching could help people plan the work of planting and harvesting.

The stars were not simply distant points of light. They were part of the way people understood the passage of time.

## When the Sky Carried Meaning

But if the sky could tell people something about the seasons, could it also offer signs about events on Earth, or even about the future?

Different cultures approached that question through their own beliefs and ways of understanding the world. In China, the connection between the sky and political order became especially important.

One remarkable record of Chinese astronomy is the Dunhuang Star Atlas, thought to have been made in the second half of the seventh century, during the Tang dynasty. The paper scroll contains thirteen charts showing more than 1,300 stars arranged into 257 groups. What makes it interesting is not only its detail, but also the names and meanings given to those groups.

The sky had counterparts to an imperial palace, advisers, soldiers, markets, and even a palace kitchen. The region around the north celestial pole was associated with the emperor and his court. As the surrounding stars appeared to turn around this region, the arrangement of the sky offered a way to picture order on Earth.

In Chinese political thought, an emperor's right to rule was associated with the Mandate of Heaven. An unexpected comet or a solar eclipse could therefore be interpreted as more than an astronomical event. It might also be read as a warning to the ruler. The sky was observed, mapped, and given meaning within the same culture.

## The Desire to Measure the Sky

Giving meaning to the stars was one thing. Knowing exactly where to find them was another. For a sailor trying to navigate open water, a scholar preparing a reliable calendar, or a traveler trying to establish a direction, an approximate idea of where the stars stood was not always enough. More accurate observations and tables were needed.

One of the most striking places where that work took place was Samarkand in the fifteenth century. Ulugh Beg, a grandson of Timur and a ruler with a deep interest in mathematics and astronomy, established an observatory there. Before telescopes, one way to make more precise measurements was to build larger instruments. The observatory's great meridian arc, with a radius of about forty metres, allowed astronomers to measure the positions of celestial objects with unusual care.

Ulugh Beg worked with scholars including Qadi Zada al-Rumi, Jamshid al-Kashi, and Ali Qushji. Their work helped produce the Zij-i Sultani, an extensive set of astronomical tables. Its star catalogue, completed around 1437, recorded the positions of 1,018 stars and became one of the major achievements of astronomy before the telescope.

Yet the catalogue was not based entirely on new observations. Twenty-seven southern stars could not be seen from Samarkand. For those, the astronomers turned to the catalogue of Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi, who had lived roughly five centuries earlier. They adjusted the older positions to account for the apparent long-term shift in the stars' coordinates. The result brought together years of new measurements and knowledge handed down across generations.

Astronomical measurement was not confined to great observatories. Portable instruments also brought this knowledge into everyday life. In 1291, a Rasulid prince in Yemen named Umar ibn Yusuf made a brass astrolabe that is now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. About sixteen centimetres across, it could help its user measure the altitude of the Sun or stars, work out the time, and determine direction. Instruments like this also served practical religious needs, including calculating prayer times and finding the direction of Mecca.

Across centuries and cultures, observations and calculations made the sky increasingly familiar. But even the most careful measurements met a limit: what the human eye could see. Astronomers could calculate with greater precision, yet countless objects beyond naked-eye vision remained unknown.

## The Stars We Could Not See

For thousands of years, people studied the sky with their eyes and the instruments they could build. In 1609, the Italian scholar Galileo Galilei improved the design of the recently invented telescope and pointed it toward the sky. The following year, in Sidereus Nuncius (The Starry Messenger), he described what he had seen.

One of his most striking observations concerned the Milky Way. To the naked eye, it appeared as a pale band stretching across the night sky. Through Galileo's telescope, it resolved into a vast gathering of faint stars. Even familiar constellations held stars that people had not been able to distinguish before. Near the stars already known in Orion, Galileo recorded about eighty more. The sky was far more crowded than it had seemed.

And the telescope revealed more than stars. Galileo observed the Moon's uneven surface, marked by mountains and depressions. In January 1610, he also watched four small bodies moving around Jupiter. These were Jupiter's moons. Their discovery challenged the idea that everything in the heavens revolved around Earth.

![Five ink drawings of the Moon in different phases, showing its uneven, cratered surface.](/essays/why-we-never-stopped-looking-at-the-stars/galileo-moon-sketches.jpg "Galileo Galilei, drawings of the Moon from Sidereus Nuncius, 1610 — Wikimedia Commons. Public Domain.")

A new instrument had brought new information, but it had also changed the questions people could ask. If so many stars lay beyond ordinary sight, how much more was there to discover? Was Earth really at the centre of everything?

The telescope was a turning point, though it did not make the work of earlier astronomers irrelevant. Galileo was looking at the same sky that observers in Mesopotamia, China, and Samarkand had studied with such patience. He could now see parts of it that had been hidden from their eyes.

In time, the ability to see farther would be joined by something earlier observers could scarcely have imagined: sending machines beyond Earth.

## From Looking to Reaching

In the second half of the twentieth century, people crossed a different boundary. They were no longer limited to studying distant objects from the ground. In 1957, the Soviet Union placed Sputnik 1 into Earth orbit. In 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space. In 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin walked on the Moon. People had reached a celestial body they had watched from afar for thousands of years.

The rapid development of rockets did not happen because of curiosity alone. Political rivalry, military goals, and economic interests all played a part in the space race. Still, space exploration gave new force to an old question: Were we alone in the universe? Could life exist elsewhere? And might we one day communicate with it?

One of the clearest expressions of that hope traveled aboard Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, launched in 1977. Their main mission was to study the outer planets. But each spacecraft also carried a gold-plated record containing sounds, music, and images from Earth. Prepared by a team led by Carl Sagan, the records included greetings in fifty-five languages, from ancient Sumerian to Turkish. They were messages for any other civilisation that might someday encounter the spacecraft.

![The gold-plated cover of the Voyager record, engraved with diagrams showing how to play it and where it came from.](/essays/why-we-never-stopped-looking-at-the-stars/voyager-golden-record.jpg "The Sounds of Earth: cover of the Voyager Golden Record, 1977 — NASA. Public Domain.")

For people who had once searched the sky for signs, there was something remarkable about sending a sign of their own into space.

The effort to travel beyond Earth continues. Projects aimed at returning people to the Moon and, eventually, sending them to Mars are trying to make space travel more sustainable. Reusable rockets are part of the attempt to reduce its technical and financial barriers. The Starship upper-stage catch in November 2026 marked a step in that work, although regular journeys to the Moon or crewed missions to Mars still face major obstacles. These are journeys within our Solar System, not voyages to the distant stars.

The reasons for looking up have changed, but the older ones have not disappeared. We still use the stars to find direction, give them meaning, and gather scientific information about them.

Far beyond Earth, the Voyager spacecraft carry two small golden records filled with voices from our planet. We do not know whether anyone will ever hear them.
